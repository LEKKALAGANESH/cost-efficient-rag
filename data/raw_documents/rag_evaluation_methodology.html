RAG Evaluation Methodology

Synthetic reference document authored for the cost-efficient-rag evaluation corpus. Contains no confidential or third-party material.

1. Why retrieval and generation are scored separately

A retrieval-augmented system has two failure modes that look identical from the outside. The answer can be wrong because the correct passage was never retrieved, or wrong because the passage was retrieved and the generator ignored or misread it. A single end-to-end score cannot distinguish these, and the remedies are opposite: the first calls for changes to chunking, embeddings, or top-k, the second for changes to the prompt or the generation model.

Our standard is therefore to score retrieval in isolation, using classical information-retrieval metrics against a labelled relevance set, and to score generation separately against the context that retrieval actually returned.

2. Retrieval metrics

2.1 Hit Rate

Hit Rate scores one for a query if any relevant chunk appears anywhere in the top-k results, and zero otherwise. It answers the question "did retrieval surface anything useful at all?" and nothing more.

2.2 Recall@k

Recall@k is the fraction of all relevant chunks that appear in the top-k results: the size of the intersection of retrieved and relevant, divided by the number of relevant chunks.

Recall@k and Hit Rate coincide only when every query has exactly one relevant chunk. As soon as any query has two or more, they diverge, and reporting Hit Rate under the label Recall@k inflates the apparent score. This is the most frequently observed error in RAG evaluation write-ups.

2.3 Mean Reciprocal Rank

For each query, the reciprocal rank is one divided by the one-indexed position of the first relevant chunk. If no relevant chunk appears in the top-k, the term contributes zero. MRR is the mean of these values across the whole question set.

Excluding zero-scoring queries from the average rather than counting them as zero is a silent inflation of the metric. A query the system failed on is a data point, not a missing value.

2.4 nDCG@k

Discounted cumulative gain at k is the sum, over ranks one through k, of the relevance grade at that rank divided by the base-two logarithm of the rank plus one. Rank one is therefore undiscounted, which is the standard convention and matches trec_eval, sklearn, and ranx.

Normalized DCG divides that quantity by the ideal DCG. The ideal DCG must be truncated at k: it sums over the first min(number of relevant chunks, k) grades sorted in descending order. Summing over all relevant chunks instead caps nDCG below one for a flawless ranking. With seven relevant chunks scored at k equals five, a perfect top-five would report approximately 0.79 rather than 1.0, and the system would appear to be failing when it is in fact optimal.

2.5 Context precision

Context precision, in its flat form, is the number of relevant chunks in the top-k divided by k. It measures what fraction of the material handed to the generator was actually useful.

This metric has a structural ceiling that must be reported alongside it. When a query has one relevant chunk and k is five, the maximum achievable precision is 0.20. A perfectly performing system reports approximately 0.19, and a reader who does not know the ceiling reads that as a failure. Always publish the achievable ceiling, computed as the mean over queries of min(number of relevant chunks, k) divided by k, and the ratio of achieved to achievable.

3. Handling unjudgeable queries

An evaluation set should contain at least one deliberately out-of-corpus question to exercise the refusal path. Such a question has an empty relevant set, which makes Recall@k a zero-divided-by-zero expression.

The rule we apply uniformly: queries with an empty relevant set are excluded from Recall@k, Hit Rate, MRR, nDCG@k, and context precision alike, and are scored only under fallback correctness. Both denominators must be reported separately so a reader can audit them. Within the judged queries, a query whose relevant chunks exist but do not appear in the top-k scores zero on all five metrics. It is scored, not excluded.

4. Answer metrics

4.1 Faithfulness

Faithfulness asks whether every factual claim in the generated answer traces to something stated in the retrieved context. It does not ask whether the claim is true in the world. An answer that confidently repeats an error present in the source documents is faithful and incorrect; those are two different axes and conflating them is the most common conceptual error in answer evaluation.

The recommended implementation extracts atomic claims from the answer, checks each against the context, and reports the ratio of supported claims to total claims.

4.2 Answer relevance

Answer relevance asks whether the answer addresses the information need actually expressed in the question. A perfectly faithful answer to a different question must score high on faithfulness and low on relevance. Keeping the two axes independent is what makes the pair diagnostic rather than decorative.

4.3 Fallback correctness

For deliberately out-of-corpus questions, the system must return its refusal string exactly and must not have invoked the generation model at all. This is verifiable from the query log: the fallback flag is set and zero completion tokens were billed.

5. Judge design

An LLM judge given a bare instruction to rate an answer from one to ten is indistinguishable from noise. Three properties make a judge usable.

First, an explicit rubric with named criteria rather than a single number. Second, a structured output schema in which the rationale field precedes the score field. Under constrained decoding a model emits fields in schema order, so placing the score first makes the rationale a post-hoc justification and voids the grounding it was meant to provide. Third, a judge drawn from a different model family than the generator, which removes the self-enhancement bias by which models systematically prefer their own family's prose.

6. Statistical power

At fifteen to thirty questions, the binomial standard error on a Recall@5 near 0.7 is approximately 0.10, so the ninety-five percent confidence interval half-width is roughly plus or minus 0.20. Two configurations differing by less than about twenty absolute points are statistically indistinguishable on a set that size.

The practical consequence is that chunk size and top-k should be fixed a priori from published defaults rather than selected against the evaluation set. Freezing the question set prevents question leakage but does nothing about model-selection overfitting, which is the actual mechanism at this sample size. Report the parameter sweep as descriptive, with confidence intervals, and retain the a-priori default.

7. Label completeness

Relevance labels written from memory over a corpus of thousands of chunks will be incomplete. Genuinely relevant chunks that were never labelled are scored as errors, biasing every metric downward by an unmeasured amount. This is the classical TREC incomplete-judgments problem.

The mitigation is pooling: run top-ten retrieval for each question under two or three configurations, take the union of retrieved identifiers as a judgment pool, and judge that pool. Chunks below pool depth are treated as non-relevant, which is the standard TREC assumption and should be stated explicitly.
